Planning your music video

Every great music video starts before a single frame is generated. Wave Cinema's director does the heavy lifting, but the plan is yours.

1. Know your song's shape

Upload your track and let the transcription map it out. Listen for the structure: intro, verse, pre-chorus, chorus, bridge, outro. Each section wants a different visual energy — the chorus should feel bigger than the verse, or the whole thing goes flat.

2. Find your story beats

A music video doesn't need a plot, but it needs beats — moments where the visuals shift. Write 3–5 in your treatment: "opens alone in neon rain," "chorus explodes into a crowd," "bridge strips back to a single close-up." The director turns these into timed scenes.

3. Write a treatment, not a script

The treatment field on the Direct stage is your pitch to the AI. Describe mood, setting, and motion — not dialogue. "Slow push through a chrome city at night, rain on glass, lonely but electric" beats a paragraph of backstory every time.

Directing scenes

The director breaks your song into 5–15 second scenes and writes a generation prompt for each. Here's the language it speaks.

Shot language

Prompts use real cinematography terms: wide shot, medium shot, close-up, extreme close-up, aerial, tracking shot, dolly-in, handheld. If you edit a prompt in Studio mode, use these words — the generator understands them.

Camera grammar

One move per scene. A slow push-in says intimacy; a fast lateral track says energy; a locked-off static frame says confidence. Mixing three camera moves in one 8-second scene reads as chaos, not dynamism.

Continuity anchors

The director writes identity locks into every prompt — palette, lighting, wardrobe, setting details. These are what keep scene 7 looking like it belongs with scene 2. Don't delete them when editing prompts in Studio.

Lyric sync

Scenes are timed to the song's word timing. A scene change landing on a downbeat or a lyric punchline feels intentional; landing mid-phrase feels like a mistake. Trust the timing, then adjust in Studio if a cut feels off.

Casting characters

Character references are how your people stay your people across every scene.

What makes a good reference

One clear, front-facing image per character. Good lighting, plain background, face clearly visible. JPG or PNG, under 10 MB. You can add up to 10 characters per project, and up to 9 reference images travel with each generated scene.

Consistency is the whole game

The generator sees your reference and the prompt's identity locks together. Name your character in the prompt ("Maya, 20s, silver jacket") and the reference handles the face. If a take comes back off-model, reroll — don't accept a stranger wearing your character's name.

When to skip characters

Abstract visuals, landscapes, and lyric videos don't need a cast. An empty cast list tells the director to go full atmosphere — sometimes that's the stronger choice.

Cutting and assembly

Generation gives you takes. Assembly gives you a film. The Review stage is where taste lives.

Pick takes like an editor

Watch every take for a scene before selecting. You're looking for the one where the motion, framing, and energy match the song section — not necessarily the prettiest frame. A slightly rough take that moves right beats a perfect still.

Reroll with intent

Rerolling re-runs the same prompt. If two rerolls miss, the prompt is the problem — edit it in Studio mode (tighten the shot language, simplify the action) before spending another generation.

Pacing

Cut on the beat. If a scene feels long, it probably is — but resist trimming scenes in a music video the way you would in narrative film. The song is the edit; your job is selecting takes that serve it.

The master

Assembly stitches your selected takes with the original song, server-side, at no extra charge. Download the MP4, watch it on a phone and a big screen, then decide if any scene needs another pass.

Aspect ratios explained

Where your video lives decides its shape. Pick before you generate — reframing after the fact always costs you composition.

  • 16:9 — Landscape. YouTube, the default. Cinematic, roomy, the safe choice for a "real" music video.
  • 9:16 — Vertical. Shorts, Reels, TikTok. Tight framing, faces and motion fill the frame. Compose close — wide shots die in vertical.
  • 1:1 — Square. Feed posts. A compromise that works everywhere and excels nowhere; good for teasers.
  • 21:9 — Ultrawide. Statement pieces. Enormous scope, but letterboxed everywhere except dedicated players.
  • 4:3 — Classic. Nostalgia, lo-fi aesthetics, archival vibes. A deliberate style choice, not a default.

Rule of thumb: generate in the ratio you'll publish in. The free Aspect Cropper can rescue a wrong choice, but a native composition always wins.
